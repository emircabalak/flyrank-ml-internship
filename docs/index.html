FlyRank ML Internship capstone · Refresh / Content Opportunity Scoring

Which already-visible pages should an editor refresh first?

Emir Çabalak · Built on the FlyRank ML Internship dataset · Repo and notebooks

Abstract

FlyRank publishes pages for many clients and cannot check every one by hand, so editors need a short list of what to fix first. I turned that into a ranking task on the anonymized internship data (30,000 pages, 32 clients): sort the visible pages by how likely each one is to be losing traffic, using only signals we would know before the drop. A plain logistic regression on those safe signals beats a fair rule-based baseline when it is tested on clients it never saw, getting about 41 of its top 50 pages right (Precision@50 of 0.81) against a base rate of 0.60. Training on the smaller pages too lifts the top of the list a little. The output is a ranked refresh list with a reason for each page. It makes no claim about Google and does not prove a refresh wins traffic back; it just helps an editor decide what to look at first.

1. The problem

A FlyRank editor looks after hundreds of live pages and has time to review maybe fifty a month. The hard part is not writing the fix, it is picking which pages deserve one. Fix a page that was fine and you waste hours and risk the ranking it already had. Miss a page that is slipping and it keeps losing traffic while no one notices. That choice is the whole case study here: take a pile of pages and turn it into an ordered list an editor can trust, with a reason next to each one.

One row is one page. The output is a ranked list with a reason and a suggested action. The person using it is an editor working from the top down. This is worth automating because the hints that a page is slipping are many and mixed together, and no single hand-written rule sorts them well. The final call still belongs to a person.

2. Data

The model, the baseline, and the testing all run on the 30,000-row anonymized slice that ships in the repo: one row per page, 32 disguised clients, numbers over the last 90 days. Keeping the work on that committed slice means anyone can rerun it without special access. My data contract checked the same setup on the full warehouse release, the March 2026 slice of about 9.8 million page-days, to make sure the shape holds at scale.

What I left out matters more than what I kept. The label comes from the trend_direction column, so that column and everything built from it stay out of the features. The 90-day and last-30-day numbers overlap the label's own window, so they are out too; only the earlier 30-day window and fixed page facts are fair to use. The client and page IDs are only for grouping and splitting, never for training. No client names, domains, links, or real search queries appear anywhere.

3. Methodology

The label is a stand-in, not a real business result: a page counts as slipping when its trend_direction reads "down". I treat it as a proxy for "worth a refresh slot" and nothing more.

The baseline is the simple CTR-fix rule from Week 4, scored here as a way to rank slipping pages, plus a plain "how stale is it" ranker as a floor. The model is logistic regression on the safe signals. A wide search under work/experiments/ compared twelve model types; the fancier trees and boosters did worse at the top of the list, and the only change that reliably helped was training on the smaller pages too while still scoring only the visible ones. Testing is 5-fold grouped by client, so no client shows up in both training and testing. Every number below is measured on clients the model never saw, with the base rate right beside it.

4. Results

Same visible pages, same client-grouped folds, same measures. The model beats both baselines, and training on the smaller pages pushes the top of the list up a bit more. The base rate for slipping pages is 0.598.

5. Limitations and honest framing

Three honest limits. First, cheating is easy here and I said no to it. The moment you feed the model the label's own time window, the score shoots toward a fake 0.9 and up: honest signals sit at 0.63, adding the 90-day window makes it 0.70, and adding the last-30-day window jumps it to 0.95. The gap between 0.63 and 0.95 is the difference between an honest model and one that has already seen the answer.

Second, a random split makes the model look better than it is. The same setup scores 0.685 when clients leak across the split versus 0.607 on clients held out, and that 0.078 gap is memorizing, not skill.

Third, and most important, this does not prove a refresh works. The model sorts slipping pages above the base rate, but whether fixing them actually wins traffic back is a different question that needs a real test this snapshot cannot run. The gain from training on smaller pages also has a wide error range, so read it as a hint, not a settled number. This is a tool to help an editor choose what to look at, and nothing more.

6. Ranked recommendations

The list turns scores into moves an editor can make tomorrow. Each of the top fifty pages gets one action and one reason. Most of the list is refreshing content and rewriting titles, with a smaller share for relevance and internal-link work.

The reasons are honest about where they run out. For nearly half of visible pages (48%) the rule finds no clear lever and says so, instead of making one up; the rest split across low visibility (25%), stale but still earning (12%), and low CTR for the position (12%). Two kinds of page fill the list: solid pages that are starting to slip, and quiet earners that pull traffic but get no attention.

7. Reproducibility

Everything reruns from a fresh clone. The capstone notebook (work/notebooks/capstone.ipynb) rebuilds every number and both charts on the committed data. The weekly notebooks (w01 through w07) hold each step, and the full model search sits under work/experiments/ with its log and sealed test set. Splits use fixed seeds. The code and setup are in the repository.
